Research Workshop: Literature Review (Students Driving, AI in the Back Seat)

Author

Camilo Calderon

Published

February 2, 2026

1 What a literature review is (and what it is not)

A literature review (LR) is a structured and critical account of what credible research has established about your topic, organised around an argument that supports your dissertation’s research objective(s).

It is not a “tour” of papers. It is a reasoned map: what we know, how we know it, what we do not know, and why your project is a logical next step.

1.1 The three jobs of a literature review

A strong LR typically does three things simultaneously:

  1. Defines the intellectual problem-space (concepts, theories, debates).
  2. Synthesises evidence and approaches (patterns, tensions, boundary conditions).
  3. Justifies your research objective(s) (gap + relevance + feasibility).
Your north star

Every paragraph should be able to answer:

“How does this revolves around or how connects with my research objective?”

1.2 What a literature review is not

  • A bibliography with sentences attached
  • A set of summaries (“Smith (2020) says…, Jones (2021) says…”)
  • A place to show you have read “a lot”
  • A proxy for your methodology or results

2 From description to analysis: the mindset shift

Students often treat literature as authority (“Paper X says Y, therefore…”).
Researchers treat literature as claims under conditions:

  • What is the claim?
  • What is the evidence and method?
  • Under what assumptions does it hold?
  • Where might it fail (context, measurement, identification, scope)?
  • How does it relate to other findings (agreement, tension, complementarity)?
  • What does this imply for my objective and design?

2.1 The “analysis verbs” that change your writing

When you write, aim to do something with the literature. Use verbs that signal analytical moves:

  • Contrasts (A emphasises X, whereas B emphasises Y)
  • Qualifies (the effect holds when / if / provided that…)
  • Interrogates (questions assumptions, measures, identification)
  • Extends (applies ideas to a new context / population / time)
  • Integrates (combines two strands into a coherent framing)
  • Disentangles (separates mechanism from correlation)
A quick self-check

If your paragraph contains only verbs like describes, reports, notes, states, you are probably summarising rather than analysing.

3 Interactive tutorial with exemplars

This workshop develops your understanding of what a literature review does, beyond summarising existing research. The focus is on critical analysis, relevance, and alignment with research objectives, using real MPhil dissertations as exemplars.

3.1 Task 1 — Spot the difference: summary vs analysis

3.1.1 Exemplar (descriptive vs analytical use of literature)

Source: Lumbe, L. (2021). The contribution of social housing as physical capital addressed in the “Smart City Wien Framework” to sustainable livelihoods.

“The definitions of ‘smart city’ could not be more different (Ponting, 2013)… Hence, there is no universally accepted description of this notion. For the purposes of this dissertation, Toli and Murtagh’s (2020) characterisation will be applied…” (Literature Review, pp. 7–8).

Why this is useful
This passage demonstrates careful coverage of definitions but only a light analytical intervention. It helps illustrate the boundary between high-quality description and explicit critical analysis.

Your task - Identify which sentences summarise existing work. - Identify where the author makes an analytical decision. - Rewrite one sentence to make the author’s judgement explicit.

3.2 Task 2 — Relevance filter (build judgement, not volume)

3.2.1 Exemplar (relevance-driven literature review)

Source: Bendavid, I. (2021). How travellers’ behaviour evolves with changes in shared e-scooter prices?

“E-scooters may be an effective solution to urban congestion and the last-mile problem. However, action must be taken for this new transport mode to better complement, rather than compete with sustainable modes of transport… This section will draw the evolution of research on micro-mobility, e-scooters and the microeconomics and marketing tools used in this paper.” (Literature Review, p. 7).

Why this is useful
The author clearly filters the literature through the research objective, demonstrating that relevance is defined by the research question, not by topic popularity.

Your task - Identify which strands of literature are prioritised. - Identify at least one plausible strand that is implicitly excluded. - Explain how this filtering supports the research objective.

3.3 Task 3 — One-sentence synthesis drill

3.3.1 Exemplar (synthesis across authors)

Source: Macpherson-Smith, E. (2021). Can urban regeneration unlock opportunities for women?

“Early feminist urban planning literature… was primarily focused on the needs of white middle-class women in the suburbs… Contemporary research led by Beebeejaun (2017) has instead attempted to reach historically marginalised women with a focus on intersectionality.” (Literature Review, pp. 7–8).

Why this is useful
This excerpt groups authors by intellectual position and evolution, rather than listing them individually. It is a clear example of synthesis.

Your task - Identify the organising principle used to group authors. - Write a one-sentence synthesis capturing the evolution of the debate. - Add a qualifier explaining why this evolution matters for the dissertation.

3.4 Task 3 — Name the gap type (stop vague “there is little literature” claims)

Choose one paragraph from your current LR draft (or a sample you provide).

Identify the most relevant gap type:

  • Theoretical gap (missing mechanism or concept)
  • Methodological gap (identification, measurement, data limitations)
  • Contextual gap (different city/country/institutional setting)
  • Temporal gap (outdated period; post-policy change)
  • Population/heterogeneity gap (who is affected; distributional effects)

Write a 3–4 line gap statement that: 1. Names the gap type, 2. Explains why it matters, 3. Links directly to your objective.

3.4.1 Exemplar (a defensible research gap)

Source: Macpherson-Smith, E. (2021). Can urban regeneration unlock opportunities for women?

“Despite this recent emergence there are still significant gaps in feminist urban planning literature, with notable omissions in the study of regeneration and the lived experiences of inner-city women.” (Ibid, p. 7).

Why this is useful
The gap is defined as a systematic omission, not an absence of literature. This makes the gap credible and defensible.

Additional task - Identify the type of gap (contextual, population, methodological). - Explain why this gap matters for the research objective. - Rewrite the gap in one sentence without using “there is little research”.

3.5 Task 5 — From literature to research design

3.5.1 Exemplar (literature review shaping method)

Source: Popham, E. (2021). Legislating for Sustainable Development Using Spatial Justice Principles.

“The aim of this literature review is to identify the spatial justice principles that are important to disrupt cycles of inequality… In order to establish an analytical framework… it is important to understand the key principles which represent spatial justice.” (Literature Review, pp. 8–9).

Why this is useful
This passage shows the literature review actively constructing the research framework, rather than serving as background.

Your task - Identify which concepts are carried forward into the framework. - Explain how theory is operationalised. - Suggest one alternative theory and how it would alter the research design.

3.6 Task 6 — Paragraph surgery (rewrite with three constraints)

Take a descriptive paragraph and rewrite it using:

  • 1 analytical verb (e.g., qualifies, contrasts, interrogates)
  • 1 explicit mechanism (the “because”)
  • 1 bridge sentence back to your objective

Keep the paragraph within 120–160 words. ::: callout-tip ## Key takeaway

A strong literature review is not about demonstrating how much you have read. It is about demonstrating judgement: what matters, why it matters, and how it justifies the research you are about to undertake. :::

3.7 Literature review “quality checklist”

Use this as a self-audit before you submit a draft.

3.8 Structure and argument

  • I have clear themes (not a chronological list of papers).
  • Each theme ends with a so what? sentence (implication for objective).
  • I have at least one explicit debate/tension (not false consensus).

3.9 Evidence and methods

  • I distinguish correlation from causal evidence where relevant.
  • I mention key methodological differences that explain disagreement.
  • I avoid over-claiming beyond the evidence.

3.10 Voice and positioning

  • I use analytical verbs and qualifiers.
  • I explain why I include sources (relevance), not just what they say.
  • I identify what my study will contribute (gap + how I address it).

3.11 A LR pipeline at a glance

When writing a literature review remain compliant with University regulations on AI use.

The guiding principle is that AI may support research and reflection, but all assessed writing must be your own original work.

The core stages are:

  • Collect widely
  • Filter systematically
  • Read deeply (a minority of papers)
  • Map concepts and gaps
  • Design structure before writing
  • Write your own content
  • Review twice: diagnostic AI checks → full human review
Non-negotiable rule (assessment context)

AI tools may be used only as an aid to research, organisation, or reflection.

Under no circumstances should AI-generated text, structure, analysis, or paraphrased output be incorporated into assessed work.

You remain fully responsible for:

  • accuracy of claims,
  • interpretation of evidence,
  • correct citation,
  • coherence and logic linking the literature to your research objective.

3.12 Step 1 — Find literature (collect widely)

3.12.1 Goal

Build a broad, traceable, and defensible pool of candidate literature before deciding what merits close reading.

3.12.2 Tools (examples)

  • Reference manager: Zotero
  • Search & discovery: Google Scholar, Elicit, Consensus, Litmaps
  • Network discovery: Connected Papers, ResearchRabbit
  • Search/reading interfaces: SciSpace

3.12.3 What to do

  • Create a dedicated reference manager collection (e.g. Dissertation – Literature Review).
  • Search using topic terms + synonyms + methodological keywords.
  • Save all potentially relevant papers at this stage (do not over-filter early).
  • Record basic metadata:
    • topic/theme,
    • methods,
    • data/context,
    • key constructs or variables.
Old-school search still matters

iDiscover, Google Scholar remains valuable when you want full control over keywords and search logic.
AI tools complement this — they do not replace it.

3.12.4 Optional exercise (planning only)

  • Draft three keyword strings:
    • core topic terms,
    • methods/approaches,
    • context (place, time, population).
  • Use them in Google Scholar and one AI-assisted search tool, then import results into your reference manager.

3.13 Step 2 — Filter literature (triage, not judgement)

3.13.1 Goal

Reduce the reading burden by identifying papers most likely to matter for your research objective.

3.13.2 Tools (examples)

  • SciSpace (relevance indicators, filters)
  • Consensus (key papers, contributors, evidence clusters)
  • NotebookLM (querying your uploaded references)

3.13.3 What to do

  • Start from the full reference manager collection.
  • Use tools to assist screening, considering:
    • topical relevance,
    • alignment with your objective,
    • foundational vs. recent contributions.
  • Create three lists:
    • papers for full reading,
    • papers to revisit later,
    • papers excluded from this project.
AI assists triage, not judgement

AI can speed up screening, but you must still check titles, abstracts, and — where needed — methods or results sections yourself.

3.14 Step 3 — Full read (deep engagement with a subset)

3.14.1 Goal

Develop genuine understanding of the field without attempting to read everything.

3.14.2 Rule of thumb

After filtering, fully read around 20–25% of the shortlisted papers (those with highest signal value).

3.14.3 During a full read, extract (in your own notes)

  • research question and motivation,
  • theoretical mechanisms,
  • data and empirical strategy,
  • strengths and limitations,
  • key findings,
  • contribution relative to the literature,
  • unresolved issues or gaps.
This step cannot be automated

Skipping full reading risks misinterpretation, weak synthesis, and factual errors — all common triggers for academic misconduct investigations.

3.15 Step 4 — Mapping concepts and gaps

3.15.1 Goal

Transform a list of papers into a structured understanding of: - key concepts, - thematic groupings, - debates, - and research gaps linked to your objective.

3.15.2 Tools (examples)

  • NotebookLM (concept mapping / visual organisation)
  • Consensus (coverage matrices and gap indicators)

3.15.3 What to do

  • Build a conceptual map based on papers you have read.
  • Group literature by:
    • concepts or mechanisms,
    • methodological approaches,
    • contexts or datasets.
  • Identify:
    • areas of dense evidence,
    • contradictions,
    • under-explored questions.

3.15.4 Deliverable

A provisional set of themes and candidate sections, written in your own words.

3.16 Step 5 — Structure (design before drafting)

3.16.1 Goal

Design the architecture of the literature review before writing prose.

3.16.2 How to use AI (permitted)

AI may be used only to prompt reflection, not to generate a structure to reuse.

Acceptable use - asking for questions you should consider, - checking whether common elements are missing.

Not acceptable - copying AI-generated outlines into assessed work.

Structure must be yours

You must be able to justify: - why sections exist, - why they appear in that order, - how each section supports the research objective.

3.16.3 Example structure (illustrative, not prescriptive)

  1. Framing the problem and relevance of the literature
  2. Core concept/theme A
  3. Core concept/theme B
  4. Methodological or measurement issues (if relevant)
  5. Debates and contested findings
  6. Research gaps and positioning of your study
  7. Summary and transition to research design

3.17 Step 6 — Content (authorial control)

3.17.1 Goal

Write the literature review entirely in your own words, grounded in sources you have read.

3.17.2 Permitted AI support

  • clarifying terminology,
  • checking logical consistency,
  • identifying where citations may be needed,
  • locating additional sources you then read yourself.

3.17.3 Writing discipline

Each paragraph should contain: - a clear claim, - supporting evidence (citations), - interpretation or comparison, - explicit relevance to the research objective.

Literature reviews are analytical, not descriptive

Avoid paper-by-paper summaries. Focus on synthesis, comparison, and evaluation.

3.18 Step 7 — Review (diagnostic AI → manual review)

3.18.1 Diagnostic AI checks (permitted)

AI may be used to flag risks, not to rewrite text.

Examples of acceptable checks: - alignment with learning outcomes, - missing citations, - unclear definitions, - logical gaps.

Treat AI feedback as a checklist

AI suggestions are prompts for your judgement — not instructions to implement verbatim.

3.18.2 Manual review (mandatory)

  • Read every sentence critically.
  • Check coherence, argument flow, and citation accuracy.
  • Proofread carefully.

3.18.3 Two manual lenses

  • Academic rigor: is the argument defensible and evidence-based?
  • Readability: is the text clear, precise, and error-free?
Peer review strengthens integrity

Feedback from supervisors, postdocs, or senior PhD students can reveal weaknesses early and reduce risk later.

3.19 Permitted prompt examples (planning and checking only)

3.19.1 Prompt A — Screening checklist

My research objective is [insert].
Based on these titles/abstracts [paste], generate a screening checklist I should apply myself when deciding what to read fully.
Do not summarise or evaluate individual papers.

3.19.2 Prompt B — Structure reflection

My research objective is [insert].
Generate questions that would help me design a logical literature review structure.
Do not propose headings or an outline.

3.19.3 Prompt C — Risk audit

Here is my draft and the marking criteria.
Identify risk areas only (e.g. unsupported claims, missing citations, unclear positioning).
Do not rewrite or suggest replacement text.

3.20 What “success” looks like

A strong literature review: - is organised around themes and mechanisms, - demonstrates critical evaluation, - clearly supports the research objective, - ends with a well-justified research gap.

3.21 Academic integrity reminder

  • AI output must never appear in assessed work (directly or paraphrased).
  • AI may be used for research assistance, planning, and self-checking only.
  • All claims must be verified against original sources.
  • You may be required to declare AI use and defend your work in a viva.
Policy takes precedence

Always follow module-specific and departmental guidance.
If in doubt, seek clarification before submission.

## Step 1 — Find literature (collect widely)

### Goal
Build a **broad, traceable, and defensible** pool of candidate literature before deciding what merits close reading.

### Tools (examples)
- **Reference manager:** Zotero  
- **Search & discovery:** Google Scholar, Elicit, Consensus, Litmaps  
- **Network discovery:** Connected Papers, ResearchRabbit  
- **Search/reading interfaces:** SciSpace  

### What to do
- Create a dedicated reference manager collection (e.g. *Dissertation – Literature Review*).
- Search using **topic terms + synonyms + methodological keywords**.
- Save all potentially relevant papers at this stage (do not over-filter early).
- Record basic metadata:
  - topic/theme,
  - methods,
  - data/context,
  - key constructs or variables.

::: callout-note
## Old-school search still matters
iDiscover, Google Scholar remains valuable when you want full control over keywords and search logic.  
AI tools complement this — they do not replace it.
:::

### Optional exercise (planning only)
- Draft three keyword strings:
  - core topic terms,
  - methods/approaches,
  - context (place, time, population).
- Use them in **Google Scholar and one AI-assisted search tool**, then import results into your reference manager.

---

## Step 2 — Filter literature (triage, not judgement)

### Goal
Reduce the reading burden by identifying papers **most likely** to matter for your research objective.

### Tools (examples)
- SciSpace (relevance indicators, filters)
- Consensus (key papers, contributors, evidence clusters)
- NotebookLM (querying your uploaded references)

### What to do
- Start from the full reference manager collection.
- Use tools to assist screening, considering:
  - topical relevance,
  - alignment with your objective,
  - foundational vs. recent contributions.
- Create three lists:
  - papers for full reading,
  - papers to revisit later,
  - papers excluded from this project.

::: callout-note
## AI assists triage, not judgement
AI can speed up screening, but you must still check titles, abstracts, and — where needed — methods or results sections yourself.
:::

---

## Step 3 — Full read (deep engagement with a subset)

### Goal
Develop genuine understanding of the field without attempting to read everything.

### Rule of thumb
After filtering, fully read **around 20–25%** of the shortlisted papers (those with highest signal value).

### During a full read, extract (in your own notes)
- research question and motivation,
- theoretical mechanisms,
- data and empirical strategy,
- strengths and limitations,
- key findings,
- contribution relative to the literature,
- unresolved issues or gaps.

::: callout-warning
## This step cannot be automated
Skipping full reading risks misinterpretation, weak synthesis, and factual errors — all common triggers for academic misconduct investigations.
:::

---

## Step 4 — Mapping concepts and gaps

### Goal
Transform a list of papers into a structured understanding of:
- key concepts,
- thematic groupings,
- debates,
- and research gaps linked to your objective.

### Tools (examples)
- NotebookLM (concept mapping / visual organisation)
- Consensus (coverage matrices and gap indicators)

### What to do
- Build a conceptual map based on papers you have read.
- Group literature by:
  - concepts or mechanisms,
  - methodological approaches,
  - contexts or datasets.
- Identify:
  - areas of dense evidence,
  - contradictions,
  - under-explored questions.

### Deliverable
A **provisional set of themes and candidate sections**, written in your own words.

---

## Step 5 — Structure (design before drafting)

### Goal
Design the **architecture** of the literature review before writing prose.

### How to use AI (permitted)
AI may be used **only to prompt reflection**, not to generate a structure to reuse.

**Acceptable use**
- asking for questions you should consider,
- checking whether common elements are missing.

**Not acceptable**
- copying AI-generated outlines into assessed work.

::: callout-important
## Structure must be yours
You must be able to justify:
- why sections exist,
- why they appear in that order,
- how each section supports the research objective.
:::

### Example structure (illustrative, not prescriptive)
1. Framing the problem and relevance of the literature  
2. Core concept/theme A  
3. Core concept/theme B  
4. Methodological or measurement issues (if relevant)  
5. Debates and contested findings  
6. Research gaps and positioning of your study  
7. Summary and transition to research design  

---

## Step 6 — Content (authorial control)

### Goal
Write the literature review **entirely in your own words**, grounded in sources you have read.

### Permitted AI support
- clarifying terminology,
- checking logical consistency,
- identifying where citations may be needed,
- locating additional sources you then read yourself.

### Writing discipline
Each paragraph should contain:
- a clear claim,
- supporting evidence (citations),
- interpretation or comparison,
- explicit relevance to the research objective.

::: callout-note
## Literature reviews are analytical, not descriptive
Avoid paper-by-paper summaries. Focus on synthesis, comparison, and evaluation.
:::

---

## Step 7 — Review (diagnostic AI → manual review)

### Diagnostic AI checks (permitted)
AI may be used to **flag risks**, not to rewrite text.

Examples of acceptable checks:
- alignment with learning outcomes,
- missing citations,
- unclear definitions,
- logical gaps.

::: callout-tip
## Treat AI feedback as a checklist
AI suggestions are prompts for your judgement — not instructions to implement verbatim.
:::

### Manual review (mandatory)
- Read every sentence critically.
- Check coherence, argument flow, and citation accuracy.
- Proofread carefully.

### Two manual lenses
- **Academic rigor:** is the argument defensible and evidence-based?  
- **Readability:** is the text clear, precise, and error-free?


::: callout-warning
## Peer review strengthens integrity
Feedback from supervisors, postdocs, or senior PhD students can reveal weaknesses early and reduce risk later.
:::

---

## Permitted prompt examples (planning and checking only)

### Prompt A — Screening checklist
> My research objective is **[insert]**.  
> Based on these titles/abstracts **[paste]**, generate a **screening checklist** I should apply myself when deciding what to read fully.  
> Do not summarise or evaluate individual papers.

### Prompt B — Structure reflection
> My research objective is **[insert]**.  
> Generate **questions** that would help me design a logical literature review structure.  
> Do not propose headings or an outline.

### Prompt C — Risk audit
> Here is my draft and the marking criteria.  
> Identify **risk areas only** (e.g. unsupported claims, missing citations, unclear positioning).  
> Do not rewrite or suggest replacement text.

---

## What “success” looks like

A strong literature review:
- is organised around **themes and mechanisms**,
- demonstrates **critical evaluation**,
- clearly supports the research objective,
- ends with a well-justified research gap.

---

## Academic integrity reminder

- AI output must **never** appear in assessed work (directly or paraphrased).
- AI may be used for research assistance, planning, and self-checking only.
- All claims must be verified against original sources.
- You may be required to declare AI use and defend your work in a viva.

::: callout-important
## Policy takes precedence
Always follow module-specific and departmental guidance.  
If in doubt, seek clarification **before** submission.
:::
